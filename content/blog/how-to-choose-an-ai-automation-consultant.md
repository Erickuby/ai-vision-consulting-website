# How to Choose an AI Automation Consultant in the UK

The useful test is whether the person in front of you can explain your current process, identify a small change worth testing, and tell you honestly when a tool, a training session or no change at all would be better.

## Quick answer

Choose an AI automation consultant who starts with your existing work, not with a tool they want to sell. A useful first engagement maps the current process, scores possible changes by time, risk and effort, and says plainly which tasks should stay with people. Many UK organisations then use a written report to decide whether a small pilot is worthwhile.

## Key takeaways

- Start with the work you already do, not with a product demonstration.
- A useful first engagement produces a written report, with human checkpoints and a clear list of what is not included.
- Training, a simple process change and consulting answer different problems. You may only need one of them.
- Fees vary by scope. There is no single UK market rate to trust.
- UK data protection and consumer rules still apply when a tool or a third party is involved. This article is not legal advice.

## What should an AI automation consultant actually do?

A competent consultant begins by mapping how a task works today: who starts it, what information is needed, where decisions happen and what goes wrong. That map separates a genuine automation opportunity from work that still needs human judgement.

Recommendations can cover rules-based automation, AI-assisted steps, or a mix of both. Some tasks should stay with people, and the review should say so. The consultant should start from the systems you already use, such as Microsoft 365, and only introduce a new tool where it clearly earns its place.

Implementation is a separate decision. A first engagement can stop at a written recommendation. If you later build or configure a workflow, that work should have its own scope, price, test plan and named owner for exceptions.

If you want a do-it-yourself method for picking a first task, see [What Should a Small Business Automate First?](/blog/what-to-automate-first-small-business). This article is about when and how to hire help.

## Do you need a consultant, training, or a simple process change?

These are easy to confuse, and a useful consultant will help you tell them apart.

**A simple process change** may be enough when the friction is unclear ownership, missing templates or a messy handover. Automating a confused process usually copies the confusion into software.

**Training** is the better first step when people already have approved tools but are not using them confidently or safely. [Practical AI training for employees](/blog/ai-training-for-employees) should leave staff able to complete an agreed task, check the result and know when not to use AI.

**Consulting** is useful when you need an independent view of which workflows are worth changing, in what order, and with what checks. It is not the same as buying a platform or booking a workshop.

If the only proposal you receive is a tool you did not ask for, you are in a sales process, not a consulting engagement.

## What should a workflow audit include?

Names vary: readiness review, opportunity assessment or discovery. The label matters less than the output. A useful first engagement should include time with the people who do the work; an inventory of repetitive tasks with time costs marked as estimates; a shortlist scored by time, risk and effort; a recommended first workflow with human checkpoints; and a written report with costed options, assumptions and a clear statement of what is not included.

### Buyer checklist

- Starts from your process, with the people who do the work, not from a pre-chosen tool.
- Ends with a written report you can share and compare, not only a verbal summary.
- Marks time figures as estimates, with assumptions stated.
- Names the human checks, and prices any build separately from the audit.
- Can proceed at first with approved, anonymised or fictional examples; does not ask for passwords.
- Leaves you able to say no, including choosing training or doing nothing.

## What questions should you ask a consultant?

A straight answer is more useful than a polished one.

1. What will you look at first, and who do you need to speak to?
2. What will we hold in our hands at the end of the first engagement?
3. Which of our current systems would you start from, and when would you add a new tool?
4. How will estimated time savings be written down, including the assumptions?
5. Where should a person still check the output before it is sent, saved or acted on?
6. What data will you need, where will it go, and which approved tools will you use?
7. If the work depends on Microsoft 365 Copilot, Power Automate or a similar product, how will you confirm the licence and any usage charges against the supplier's current terms? Those costs are separate from a consulting fee.
8. What is not included in the fee, who owns the report, and what would make you advise us not to automate this?

## How do you read a proposal, and what are the red flags?

A proposal should be specific enough that two people in your organisation would describe the same piece of work. Look for a defined activity, a written deliverable, a timetable, a price and a list of exclusions.

Treat these as reasons to slow down or walk away:

- Guaranteed savings with no baseline and no method for measurement.
- No written report, or a tool chosen before the work has been mapped.
- A request for passwords, or for live confidential files, when anonymised or fictional examples would do for a first review.
- No named human review on work that will be sent to a customer, a member of staff or a decision-maker.
- A claim that UK data protection or consumer rules do not apply because a supplier is involved.
- Pressure to buy software, licences or a long programme before you have seen the first findings.

You remain responsible for what an automated or AI-assisted process does, including when a third party designs or runs it.

## What UK rules should a consultant help you think about?

This is general information, not legal advice. Your organisation remains responsible for its own compliance, and some situations need a specialist adviser.

If a workflow will process personal data, the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 still apply. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes [guidance on AI and data protection](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/). That guidance is under review because of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, so check the ICO page for updates.

For decisions taken on or after 5 February 2026, section 80 of that Act changed the UK GDPR rules on significant decisions based solely on automated processing. Organisations may make such decisions in a wider range of situations than before, but they must put safeguards in place: information about the decision, a way to make representations, a way to obtain human intervention, and a way to contest it. A decision is solely automated if there is no meaningful human involvement. Special category data remains more restricted. The ICO summarises the changes, and the commencement regulations confirm the date.

If customers will interact with an AI agent, or receive information it produces, consumer law still applies. GOV.UK guidance on [complying with consumer law when using AI agents](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/complying-with-consumer-law-when-using-ai-agents/complying-with-consumer-law-when-using-ai-agents), updated 30 October 2026, says you are responsible for what the agent does even if someone else designed or provides it. Tell customers where that is needed to avoid misleading them, and keep a person checking the output.

The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology published an [AI Risk Management Toolkit](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-risk-management-toolkit) on 8 September 2026. It is a starting point, not a certificate. In February 2026 DSIT also said it will not publish the proposed AI Management Essentials tool and will not make it a government procurement requirement. You still need your own rules for approved tools, data and review. A [policy and governance package](/services/ai-policy-and-governance/) is a different piece of work from an audit.

## When do you not need a consultant yet?

You may not need to hire anyone yet if you cannot name the task, the people who do the work have not been asked what slows them down, training on tools you already have would address the problem, the process is still changing every week, or nobody owns exceptions and review.

In those cases, tidy the process, book training, or have a short discovery conversation first. A consultant who tells you to wait is more useful than one who sells you a build you are not ready to run.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does an AI automation consultant do?

They map how a task is done today, find the repetitive steps that cost time, and recommend which to automate, which to support with AI and which to leave with people. They should also help you decide whether a small pilot is worthwhile before anything larger is built.

### How much does an AI workflow audit cost in the UK?

Fees vary by firm and scope, and there is no reliable published average. AI Vision Consulting's audit starts at £950, including half a day and a written report with costed options. The fee is credited in full against a workshop or programme booked within 60 days. No VAT is added. Implementation is quoted separately.

### Do we need an audit before booking training?

Not always. Training helps people use approved tools well. An audit asks which of your workflows is worth changing. If staff mainly need practice, start with training. If you are unsure what to change first, start with a short discovery call or an audit.

### Will the consultant need access to our live systems and confidential files?

A first audit should usually be possible with the people who do the work and with approved, anonymised or fictional examples. If real documents later become necessary, classify them first, minimise the data, and use only approved tools. Do not hand over passwords.

### What should stay with people?

Decisions that affect customers, staff, money or legal obligations, and any output that will be sent or published, should have a named human review. Exceptions and complaints also need an owner.

### How do we know whether the work was worthwhile?

Agree a baseline first. Track a few measures such as handling time, missed steps, exceptions and checking time, and review actual records. A faster process is not better if it creates inaccurate data or confusing messages.
